The model is the object of inference, and the average treatment effect is an accounting identity: an average of fitted contrasts over a population that the analyst chooses. The chapter develops that view. A statistical model is a family of processes that must be consistent under restriction of the sample, and the average effect is a functional of the fitted model and a covariate law. Noncollapsibility of the odds ratio shows the gap between the model and its accounting even under randomization, and the choice of scale for logistic regression changes what an average means. Randomization makes the assignment ignorable, so the outcome model need not model who assigned the treatment. The Lanarkshire milk experiment shows a case where the assignment was not ignorable and no model of the recorded variables could repair it. The chapter also asks what this view does to double machine learning.

## From the literature

The chapter presents or builds on the statistical model as a process (McCullagh 2002), noncollapsibility of the odds ratio (Gail, Wieand and Piantadosi 1984; Greenland, Robins and Pearl 1999), the Lanarkshire milk experiment and its critique (Student 1931), randomization and ignorability (Rubin 1978).
